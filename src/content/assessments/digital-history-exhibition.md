**Worth 50% of the course mark. Due Friday 11 May 2035 at 5 pm, Canberra time.**

## The question

**Why do people continue to enter, inhabit and protect virtual worlds after
the SAO incident?**

Make a small exhibition for a visitor who has not taken the course. Select
and arrange evidence so that the visitor encounters an argument about that
question. You might trace changing ideas of safety, relationships across
worlds, or who gets to decide which lives matter. Choose a focus that the
items can support.

Use **slides, a document or a simple webpage**. All receive the same marking criteria;
programming is optional and carries no separate credit. You are curating a
historical exhibition, not designing a new course website.

## Select six items

Choose **two items from each act** of the [course outline](/#weeks):

- **Act I, Weeks 1–4:** entering a world; promise, crisis and everyday society.
- **Act II, Weeks 5–8:** reasons to return; relationships, access and belonging.
- **Act III, Weeks 9–12:** lives and legacies; bodies, artificial life and conflict.

An item can be a short sourced passage, an optional still, an annotated event
record, or a timeline or diagram you make from named sources. Images and
video are not required. A diagram is your interpretation of evidence; explain
its basis rather than presenting it as a recovered historical object.

Use the course's onsite case accounts and named optional material. An item
may draw on more than one source, and one source may support more than one
item if each does different work. Do not split one passage into several items
just to meet the count. Label any classroom reconstruction and distinguish
an account's report from your interpretation. The required case texts are
available in full on the site.

## Write for a first-time visitor

Give each item a title and a **60–100 word caption**. The caption should orient
the visitor, interpret a specific detail and explain why this item belongs in
your argument. Attach a case-file, account and paragraph reference to it. Use supported
event dates or a clearly labelled relative sequence; mark uncertainty instead
of supplying a precise date the account does not give.

Add a **300–400 word curatorial argument** that answers the question, explains
your selection and sequence, and addresses a competing perspective or a
limit in the evidence. It can introduce the exhibition or follow the items.
You do not need to settle every controversy. Explain why your particular
route through the material is worth taking.

The six captions and argument total **660–1000 words**. Quoted words count
within these limits; item titles, citation labels, reference entries, alt
text and the assistance note do not. Alt text should provide access to an
image's relevant information, not hide extra assessed analysis.

## What to hand in

Use the [course hand-in procedure](/policies/#handing-in-work-and-receiving-feedback).
It gives the delivery arrangements and when feedback returns.

Choose one route:

- **Slides or document:** submit an accessible PDF export or DOCX with all six
  items, complete captions and argument in reading order. Keep text selectable
  and images large enough to inspect. Speaker notes must not contain essential
  material absent from the submitted reading copy.
- **Webpage:** submit a self-contained HTML file, or one ZIP containing
  `index.html` and its local assets. It must open without a login, account or
  network connection. Keep all essential text readable without animation or
  interaction; use descriptive headings and link labels.

Include your student identifier, caption and argument word counts, a complete
reference list, media credits and an assistance note. Use the filename
`exhibition-[student-id]` with the appropriate extension. Public hosting is
not required, and a link to a private editing workspace is not a submission.

Credit the creator and source of every item, including screenshots and images.
For your own diagrams, name yourself as maker and cite the evidence underneath.
For compiled accounts, name the course team, case file, account and paragraph
ID; add its URL and your access date to the reference list. Shared preparation
does not make the accounts independent corroboration. If you use optional
material, identify its creator and exact reading location as well.

Use text descriptions or alt text for meaningful images and adequate contrast
for reading. A text-led exhibition can meet every criterion.

## What the criteria mean

- **Curatorial argument, 35%:** a clear answer develops across the exhibition
  and connects the three acts without treating everyone as having one motive.
- **Source interpretation, 25%:** details are accurate and traceable, with
  account focus, evidential limits and event chronology handled carefully.
- **Selection and communication, 20%:** each item contributes something
  distinct; the sequence and captions make the reasoning usable for a newcomer.
- **Perspectives and limits, 10%:** the exhibition takes a competing account
  seriously and makes consequential uncertainty visible.
- **Access and credits, 10%:** a reader can reach all assessed content, follow
  its order and identify the sources and assistance used.

## Build it over four weeks

In Week 9, select a provisional set of items. In Week 10, test one caption and
your central claim with a peer. In Week 11, assemble a complete reading draft,
using feedback from the [Incident study](/assessments/incident-study/). Plan
separate work blocks for selection, writing and revision rather than leaving
formatting and source checking until the deadline.

Bring the draft to the **Week 12 peer review**. Ask a reader to explain your
argument back to you and identify an item whose role is unclear. Use the
**final three days before submission** to revise the sequence, check every
citation and read the whole exhibition in its submitted format. Peer feedback
is preparation, not a separate marked task.
